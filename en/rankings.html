Open Access in Institutional Rankings

Author
Affiliation

Sophia Dörner

Göttingen State and University Library

Institutional or university rankings attract a great deal of attention in academic policy circles and amongst the general public because they provide a clear comparison of academic institutions on a global scale. They serve various stakeholder groups, such as policy-makers, university managers and prospective students, acting, amongst other things, as a tool for strategic decision-making, for illustrating institutional excellence, or for choosing a place to study (Johnes, 2018; Waltman et al., 2012). The methodology on which the rankings are based is sometimes subject to numerous criticisms, for example because of the tendency to summarise multiple dimensions of academic institutions’ performance into a single, often untransparent score (Diprose et al., 2023; Piro & Sivertsen, 2016; van Raan, 2005). This makes it difficult not only to interpret the results, but also fails to take sufficient account of the different missions and priorities of the institutions (Huang, Neylon, Brookes-Kenworthy, et al., 2020; Waltman et al., 2012).

In recent years, the inclusion of open access metrics in university rankings has gained in importance. Open access refers to free and unrestricted online access to research results (Suber, 2012) and has become an important indicator of an academic institution’s commitment to open and transparent scientific practice. This trend is further supported by the requirements of funding organizations, which are increasingly viewing open access publishing as a condition for research funding (Deutsche Forschungsgemeinschaft, 2025; Fournier, 2017; Huang, Neylon, Hosking, et al., 2020b; National Institutes of Health, 2024; Rücknagel, 2021; Wellcome, 2025). In addition to the many institutions’ own commitment to open access, these requirements provide a further incentive to make research findings available via open access. The inclusion of open access indicators in university rankings makes it possible to assess institutions’ open access activities and provides an additional dimension for comparison (Diprose et al., 2023).

Following the argument of Moed (2016), it is assumed that a comparative analysis of several ranking systems can help users better understand, interpret, and use the information provided by these systems. This also applies to the open access dimension of rankings. When rankings are used to compare institutional open access engagement or to monitor corresponding activities, the informative value of ranking results depends substantially on how strongly the underlying ranking methodologies differ. To date, however, no systematic investigation appears to exist that specifically compares the open access dimension across several ranking systems.

In this study, institutional rankings with open access indicators were identified via a web search. Rankings were included if they met three criteria: (1) they constitute an institutional-level ranking (as opposed to, e.g., journal- or researcher-level rankings), (2) they include at least one open access indicator, and (3) they have a global geographic scope. Based on these criteria, five institutional rankings with open access as a comparative dimension could be identified: the Leiden Ranking 2025 (LR), the Leiden Ranking Open Edition 2025 (LROE), the SCImago Institutions Ranking 2025 (SIR), the Quacquarelli Symonds World University Rankings: Sustainability 2025 (QS), and the COKI OA Dashboard 2025 (COKI). All five rankings contain one or more open access indicators and are globally oriented in terms of their geographic coverage.

This page provides an overview of the characteristics of the five ranking systems, as well as an analysis of the extent to which the ranking results are comparable with one another. This includes, amongst other things, a comparison of the data sources and open-access indicators used by the rankings, as well as an assessment of the extent to which institutions can participate in the data collection carried out by the ranking providers. The focus here is particularly on the open access dimension.

Institutional Rankings with Open Access as a Comparative Dimension

The following sections begin with a description of the core characteristics of the selected rankings. At the end of each section, details of the rankings are summarised in a drop-down text box headed ‘Ranking Details’. This includes, in particular, a comprehensive list of the open access indicators used in each ranking system.

Leiden Ranking

The Leiden Ranking has been produced annually since 2007 by the Centre for Science and Technology Studies (CWTS) at Leiden University in the Netherlands (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, n.d.-i; Waltman et al., 2012). The ranking does not provide a traditional league table based on a single overall score, but rather presents various bibliometric indicators across the dimensions of scientific impact, collaboration, Open Access and gender. Indicators are presented in both size-dependent (absolute numbers) and size-independent (relative shares) form to account for differences in university size. The ranking also provides trend analyses over time and stability intervals to reflect statistical uncertainties (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, n.d.-c; Van Eck, 2025c).

The 2025 edition of the Leiden Ranking includes bibliometric data for 1,594 universities worldwide (Van Eck, 2025c). Indicators are reported for four-year periods; the 2025 edition extends these to include the period 2020–2023 (Van Eck, 2025c). Bibliographic data is retrieved from the Web of Science, however, only articles and reviews published in international scientific journals are taken into account (Van Eck, 2025c). Universities are selected on the basis of their research output, and must have at least 800 so-called core publications to be included in the ranking. Core publications are defined as publications that have appeared in international scientific journals in fields suitable for citation analysis. These publications must be written in English, have at least one author, must not have been retracted, and must have appeared in a so-called core journal. Core journals are defined through their international focus and must be situated in a field suitable for citation analysis. The Leiden Ranking does not use data provided directly by universities (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, n.d.-g; Van Eck, 2025c).

A key characteristic of the Leiden Ranking is its focus on open access publications. Indicators relating to open access publishing were added in the 2019 edition of the ranking (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, n.d.-i). To determine the open access status of publications, the Leiden Ranking uses OpenAlex as its data source (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, n.d.-e; Van Eck, 2025c).

Leiden Ranking Open Edition

The Leiden Ranking Open Edition has been compiled annually since 2024 (Waltman et al., 2024) by the Centre for Science and Technology Studies (CWTS) at Leiden University in the Netherlands (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, n.d.-j). It is a near reproduction of the traditional Leiden Ranking using open data sources (Waltman et al., 2024) and, like the traditional ranking, does not provide a conventional league table, but rather various bibliometric indicators across the dimensions of scientific impact, collaboration and Open Access. In the Open Edition, the indicators are also presented in both size-dependent (absolute figures) and size-independent (relative shares) form to account for differences in the size of universities. Additionally, the Open Edition of the Leiden Ranking also provides trend analyses over time and stability intervals to address statistical uncertainties (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, n.d.-d).

The 2025 edition of the Leiden Ranking Open Edition introduces two changes compared to the 2024 edition: Firstly, in addition to core publications, so-called non-core publications are included in the preparation of the ranking. Secondly, unlike the 2024 edition and the traditional Leiden Ranking, bibliometric data from a larger number of universities is included (van Eck et al., 2025). Whereas the 2024 version comprised bibliometric data for 1,506 universities worldwide (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, n.d.-a), the 2025 version is based on bibliometric data from 2,831 universities (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, n.d.-h; van Eck et al., 2025). Indicators are reported for four-year periods; the 2025 version extends these to include the period 2020–2023 (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, n.d.-d). Bibliographic data are retrieved from OpenAlex, and the respective indicators are calculated and reported separately for the set of core publications and for the combined set of core publications and non-core publications. The definition of core publications and core journals in the Leiden Ranking Open Edition is largely identical to that in the traditional Leiden Ranking (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, n.d.-c, n.d.-d). However, the Open Edition expands the set of core publications to include book chapters published in book series. Furthermore, all publications must contain not only author details but also affiliation details and references in order to be counted as core publications (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, n.d.-d). Less restrictive criteria apply to non-core publications; for example, the restriction to English-language publications does not apply, and non-core publications may also have been published outside core journals, such as in journals with a national or regional focus, books, book series, or conference proceedings (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, n.d.-d).

The Leiden Ranking Open Edition also focuses on open access publications and offers largely the same open access indicators as the traditional Leiden Ranking, with the exception of the indicators relating to publications with an unclear open access status (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, n.d.-d). Furthermore, a distinctive feature of the Leiden Ranking Open Edition is that the publications underlying the indicators can be viewed via the web interface (van Eck, Waltman, et al., 2024).

SCImago Institutions Ranking

The SCImago Institutions Ranking has been compiled annually by the SCImago Research Group since 2009 (Rousseau et al., 2018). The ranking provides a traditional league table of academic and research-oriented institutions based on a composite indicator on a scale of 0 to 100. The composite indicator combines assessments across three dimensions: research, innovation and societal impact. The composite indicator is derived from a weighted combination of metrics across these dimensions, using both size-dependent and size-independent indicators. For each institution, the ranking also provides a series of charts, for example showing changes in ranking over time, comparisons at national, regional or global level, or similar institutions based on their publication profile (SCImago Research Group, n.d.-a).

The 2025 version of the SCImago Institutions Ranking includes data for 9,755 institutions worldwide (SCImago Research Group, n.d.-b). The composite indicator is reported on an annual basis for a five-year period ending two years prior to the publication of the ranking; for the 2025 edition, this covers the period 2019–2023. Bibliographic data are retrieved from Scopus. Institutions are selected based on their publication output. In the final year of the period under review, institutions must have published at least 100 publications indexed in Scopus in order to be included in the ranking. Furthermore, at least 75% of an institution’s total publication output must consist of so-called citable items. For the SCImago Institutions Ranking, document types counted as citable items include articles, books, book chapters, conference papers, data papers, editorials, letters, notes, reviews and short surveys. (Elsevier, 2026; SCImago Research Group, n.d.-a).

Within the research dimension, which accounts for 50% of the composite indicator, an open access indicator was added to the ranking in the 2019 version. With a weighting of 2% within the research dimension, this indicator measures the percentage of documents published in open access journals or indexed in Unpaywall. To determine the open access status of publications, the SCImago Institutions Ranking uses Unpaywall as its data source (SCImago Research Group, n.d.-a).

QS World University Rankings: Sustainability

The QS World University Rankings: Sustainability has been compiled annually by Quacquarelli Symonds since 2023, following the publication of a pilot version in 2022 (Quacquarelli Symonds, 2024). The ranking provides a traditional league table of higher education institutions based on a composite indicator for both the individual categories and sub-categories (Lenses), as well as an overall score. The category scores are displayed for all institutions assessed, whereas the overall scores are only shown up to a certain point and are then hidden (Quacquarelli Symonds, n.d.-a). The QS World University Rankings: Sustainability assesses institutions across three dimensions: Environmental Impact, Social Impact and Governance.

The 2025 edition of the QS World University Rankings: Sustainability includes data on 1,744 higher education institutions worldwide (Quacquarelli Symonds, 2024). Bibliographic data are retrieved from Scopus and cover a 5-year period (Quacquarelli Symonds, n.d.-d). Only the following document types are taken into account: article, review, conference paper, book, book chapter, article in press, business article, data paper and report (Quacquarelli Symonds, n.d.-e, n.d.-f). Institutions are only included in the QS World University Rankings: Sustainability if, on the one hand, they meet the eligibility criteria for inclusion in at least one of the QS World University Rankings, QS Rankings by Region or QS Rankings by Subject, and, on the other hand, they achieve a non-zero score for specific indicators within the Environmental Impact and Social Impact dimensions. (Quacquarelli Symonds, n.d.-g).

Within the Governance dimension, which accounts for 10% of the total score, one indicator relates to open access publications. This indicator is weighted at 1% within the Governance dimension and measures the share of open access publications in the total publication volume. This proportion is calculated separately for each of the six fields considered and aggregated into a weighted total. Gold and hybrid publications are given greater weight in the calculation than publications published via the green open access route. To determine the open access status of publications, the QS World University Rankings: Sustainability uses Unpaywall as its data source (Quacquarelli Symonds, n.d.-b).

COKI OA Dashboard

The COKI Open Access Dashboard has been produced by the Curtin Open Knowledge Initiative since 2022 (Curtin Open Knowledge Initiative, 2022; Curtin Open Knowledge Initiative & Wilson, 2022). The dashboard is not a traditional ranking, but institutions and countries can be sorted by their proportion of open access publications, as well as by their total and open access publication volumes (Curtin Open Knowledge Initiative, 2022). The dashboard also shows, for each country and institution, the platform through which open access publications are made available, both as an aggregate figure covering the entire publication period and broken down by individual publication years (Curtin Open Knowledge Initiative, 2022).

The 18 August 2025 version of the COKI Open Access Dashboard includes data for 227 countries and 56,187 institutions worldwide (Curtin Open Knowledge Initiative, n.d.-c). Bibliographic data is retrieved from several open data sources and covers the publication period 2000–2024; Crossref is used in particular to identify publications (Curtin Open Knowledge Initiative, n.d.-c, n.d.-d). The selection of document types is less restrictive than in the other ranking systems; the only exclusions are publications classified as datasets, databases, components, report components, peer reviews, funding awards, conference proceedings, journal issues, report series, book catalogues, and all publications without a specified document type. (Curtin Open Knowledge Initiative, n.d.-c). Institutions must have at least 50 publications to be included in the COKI Open Access Dashboard, unless they were already included in the dataset when it was still based on Microsoft Academic Graph (Curtin Open Knowledge Initiative, n.d.-c).

The COKI Open Access Dashboard focuses exclusively on the open access activities of institutions and countries. Whereas the list view primarily displays the absolute number and the percentage of open access publications, detailed views of individual institutions and countries provide more nuanced breakdowns of publication activities and locations, supplemented by visualisations (Curtin Open Knowledge Initiative, n.d.-d). To determine the open access status of publications, the COKI Open Access Dashboard uses Unpaywall as its data source (Curtin Open Knowledge Initiative, n.d.-c).

Comparability of Ranking Results

In order to assess how comparable the results of the selected rankings are, the subsequent sections focus on examining the following aspects: the number and overlap of institutions featured in all rankings, the geographical distribution of institutions across countries and regions of the world, the data sources used by the ranking systems, the open access indicators used by the rankings and their weighting, and the extent to which institutions can participate in the data collection process carried out by the ranking providers.

The aim is to examine the extent to which the individual ranking systems differ and what implications this may have for the use of a particular ranking in open access monitoring activities or strategic decision-making.

Institutional Overlap

To determine the institutional overlap between the rankings, first available ranking data was downloaded1. In addition to the names and locations (country details, and in some cases details of regions and sub-regions) of all institutions, data from both versions of the Leiden Ranking and from the COKI OA Dashboard included identifiers from the Research Organisation Registry (ROR). Furthermore, data from the COKI OA Dashboard and the SCImago Institutions Ranking included sector classifications for all institutions represented in the rankings.

In order to uniquely match identical institutions across rankings using their ROR IDs, the data of the SCImago Institutions Ranking and the QS Sustainability Ranking first had to be supplemented with ROR IDs. For this purpose, the complete ROR dataset in version 2.9 (Research Organization Registry, 2026) was downloaded. Subsequently, an automated matching based on institutional name and country information was performed against the ROR data for both rankings. The QS data contained entries for a total of 1,743 institutions2, of which 502 institutions could not be assigned ROR IDs via the automated matching procedure.

It proved even more difficult to map ROR IDs to the 9,755 institutions included in the SCImago Institutions Ranking3. Based on the automated matching process using the institutional name and country details, no ROR IDs could be assigned to 5,065 institutions. This may be partly due to the fact that the SCImago Institutions Ranking also lists institutions that are classified as multinational (SCImago Research Group, n.d.-a). This appears to affect, in particular, companies that operate on a supra-regional level, such as Google. Although ROR also provides IDs for companies (see Google), these appear to be assigned to specific countries. However, the greater difficulty for automated matching is likely to be the differences in naming conventions between the SCImago Institutions Ranking and ROR4. To increase coverage with ROR IDs for the SCImago Institutions Ranking, a fuzzy matching approach was tested, which also took into account alternative name forms and acronyms from the ROR dataset. However, the result was not sufficiently accurate and would have required a significant amount of data cleaning. Overall, this represents a significant limitation on the usability of the SCImago dataset for a global analysis of institutional overlap.

Due to the limitations in assigning ROR IDs, it was decided to restrict the analysis of institutional overlap to German institutions. In the QS Sustainability Ranking dataset, a total of 50 institutions from Germany were identified, all of which could be assigned a ROR ID, either via automated matching or manual additions. In the SCImago Institutions Ranking dataset, a total of 364 German institutions were identified, of which 342 could not be assigned a ROR ID via automated matching. A manual assignment was carried out for these 342, however, no ROR ID could be assigned to 5 of these institutions as they have no record in ROR. Consequently, 385 institutions were included in the analysis of institutional overlap among German institutions for the SCImago Institutions Ranking.

Figure 1 quantifies the institutional overlap between German institutions across the various ranking systems. The figure only includes those institutions to which ROR IDs could be assigned.

In total, after matching the ROR IDs 2,928 unique institutions from Germany were identified across all ranking systems. As can be seen from Figure 1, the COKI OA Dashboard covers significantly more German institutions than all other ranking systems, both in total (2,927 institutions, 99.97%), as well as in terms of the number of institutions not included in any other ranking system (2,570 institutions, 87.77%). This could be attributed in particular to the fact that the COKI OA Dashboard, while covering a relatively long publication period (2000–2024), also includes institutions from multiple sectors, as well as institutions with a relatively low publication volume – typically at least 50 publications (Curtin Open Knowledge Initiative, n.d.-c, n.d.-b).

Figure 1 also shows that, of the 358 institutions included in the SCImago Institutions Ranking, 1 institutions (0.03%) are not included in any other ranking. By contrast, all institutions from both versions of the Leiden Ranking and from the QS Sustainability Ranking are also featured in at least one other ranking. In total, there are only 47 institutions (1.61%) included in all five ranking systems.

Whereas the QS Sustainability Ranking and both versions of the Leiden Ranking include only higher education institutions in their rankings, the COKI OA Dashboard and the SCImago Institutions Ranking also cover institutions from other sectors. Higher education institutions are classified as Education in the COKI dataset, whilst in the SCImago Institutions Ranking dataset, the Universities sector is assigned to higher education institutions. In order to subsequently examine the institutional overlap of German higher education institutions across the five ranking systems, a sector column was added to the consolidated dataset of all ranking systems. For institutions featured in both versions of the Leiden Ranking or in the QS Sustainability Ranking, the sector classification Universities was added. For institutions featured in the SCImago Institutions Ranking or the COKI OA Dashboard, the original classification was retained. The dataset was then filtered to include only those institutions to which the values Universities or Education had been assigned in the sector column.

Figure 2 below shows the institutional overlap between the rankings with regard to German universities. The figure also includes only those institutions to which ROR IDs could be assigned.

In total, 395 different German higher education institutions were identified across all ranking systems. Figure 2 shows that the COKI OA Dashboard also covers significantly more German higher education institutions than any other ranking system, both in total (395 institutions, 100%), as well as in terms of the number of institutions not included in any other ranking system (287 institutions, 72.66%). According to the Federal Statistical Office, a total of 422 German higher education institutions were recorded in the 2024/2025 winter term (Statistisches Bundesamt, 2025). Thus, the COKI OA Dashboard currently represents 93.6% of all German higher education institutions. It is also the only ranking system that includes higher education institutions not featured in any other ranking. The total number of German higher education institutions featured in all five ranking systems, with 47 institutions, remains unchanged from the total number of all German institutions featured in all five ranking systems.

The COKI OA Dashboard and the SCImago Institutions Ranking are the only two of the ranking systems examined that do not limit their rankings to higher education institutions. With regard to Germany, of the total 2,927 German institutions featured in the COKI OA Dashboard, 13.5% are German higher education institutions. By comparison, of the total 358 German institutions featured in the SCImago Institutions Ranking, 30.17% are German higher education institutions. For both ranking systems, it can therefore be observed that the majority of the German institutions included are assigned to other sectors.

Geographical Distribution

Figures 3 and 45 below show the geographical distribution of the institutions in terms of the countries and regions of the world in which they are located. Although all five rankings have a global focus, the figures are intended to visualise whether there are differences in geographical distribution between the rankings. To ensure better comparability, the analysis has been restricted to those institutions classified under the higher education sector (Universities, Education).

Looking at the absolute numbers of higher education institutions, Figures 3 and 4 show in particular that, for both versions of the Leiden Ranking and the SCImago Institutions Ranking, the majority of higher education institutions are based in China (LR: institutions, LROE: institutions, SIR: institutions). By contrast, for the QS Sustainability Ranking and the COKI OA Dashboard, most institutions are based in the United States of America (QS: institutions, COKI: institutions). Furthermore, the figures illustrate that, with the exception of the COKI OA Dashboard, certain countries, particularly those in the Global South, are not represented at all in the ranking systems.

In addition to the absolute number of higher education institutions per country and ranking system, below we examine the over- or under-representation of countries within the ranking systems using the preference as defined by Moed (2016). Moed (2016) defines preference as6:

The preference of ranking system R for a particular country C is expressed as the ratio of the actual and the expected number of institutions from C appearing in R, where the expected number is based on the total number of institutions across countries and across systems, under the assumption of independence of these two variables. A value of 1.0 indicates that the number of institutions from C in R is ‘as expected’.

The resulting preference score therefore measures whether a country, in terms of the number of institutions based there, is over- or under-represented within a ranking system. A value of 1 is neutral, i.e. the number of institutions from a country in a ranking system corresponds to expectations. Values above 1 indicate that more institutions from a country are included in a ranking system than expected. Values below 1 indicate that fewer institutions from a country are included in a ranking system than expected.

Table 1 shows, for all five ranking systems, the top five countries as measured by preference score. It displays the absolute number of higher education institutions and the respective preference score for each country, and additionally includes the values for Germany.

Table 1 reveals considerable differences in the geographical over-representation of the ranking systems. The traditional Leiden Ranking features a diverse range of countries without a consistent regional pattern (Luxembourg, Malta, China, Australia, Spain). The Leiden Ranking Open Edition features a greater number of countries from the Global South, as well as a few other countries outside the established academic centres of North America and Western Europe (Grenada, Cameroon, South Africa, Turkey, Italy), whilst the QS Sustainability Ranking focuses predominantly on Asian countries and territories (Brunei, Hong Kong, Saudi Arabia, Fiji, Azerbaijan). The regional focus of the Leiden Ranking Open Edition suggests an effect resulting from the inclusion of so-called non-core publications as well as a lower publication threshold. This expansion specifically captures more universities from countries such as India, Indonesia and Brazil, which are under-represented in the traditional Leiden Ranking (van Eck et al., 2025). In the SCImago Institutions Ranking and the COKI OA Dashboard, the top five countries each share an identical preference score (2.69 and 1.70 respectively). This is due to the fact that the respective countries are represented exclusively in their respective ranking systems. It is also striking that the top five countries in the SCImago Institutions Ranking are represented by just a single institution each.

With regard to Germany, Table 1 presents a mixed picture. Whilst the traditional Leiden Ranking (1.44), the QS Sustainability Ranking (1.14) and the Leiden Ranking Open Edition (1.02) show a slightly above-average representation of German universities, the COKI OA Dashboard (0.99) is close to the expected value and the SCImago Institutions Ranking (0.85) is even slightly below it. Germany is therefore not among the top five countries in any of the five systems.

Data Sources

This section examines the data sources used by the five rankings, focusing on the following two categories of data sources: 1) data sources used to obtain bibliographic data, and 2) data sources used to determine open access status. Differences in the coverage, completeness and methodology of these data sources can lead to discrepancies in the calculation of metrics and thus limit the comparability of results across different rankings. Furthermore, the study examines whether the data sources used result in disciplinary differences between the rankings.

Bibliographic Data Sources

Huang, Neylon, Hosking, et al. (2020a) identify two key sources of information that are necessary for the analysis of research outputs. Firstly, information sources that provide data to link research outputs to the relevant units of analysis (e.g. universities, research institutions). Secondly, information sources that provide data to link research outputs to relevant metrics (e.g. open access status) (Huang, Neylon, Hosking, et al., 2020a). In the context of compiling university rankings, bibliographic data sources play a central role in providing this data and are essential for a quantitative assessment of the respective productivity of the universities under consideration.

As Table 2 shows, the five rankings examined use four different bibliographic data sources: Web of Science (WoS), Scopus, Crossref and OpenAlex. Of these, Web of Science (WoS) is the most established abstract and citation database (Birkle et al., 2020). Until the mid-2000s, WoS was the leading tool for bibliometric analyses (Gauch, 2023). In 2004, Elsevier launched Scopus, the first comparable multidisciplinary database, which broke WoS’s previous monopoly (Baas et al., 2020; Gauch, 2023; Huang, Neylon, Brookes-Kenworthy, et al., 2020). Crossref was established in 2000 as a membership-based DOI registration agency. Publishers and other publication infrastructures can register DOIs for their publications via Crossref and, at the same time, submit publication metadata to the service. Since 2013, Crossref has offered a REST API through which Crossref’s publication metadata is openly accessible and can be reused (Hendricks et al., 2020). In 2022, OpenAlex was introduced as an open data source, with the aim of succeeding the discontinued Microsoft Academic Graph (MAG) as a fully open source of research information (Priem et al., 2022). Since its launch, OpenAlex has established itself as a serious alternative to proprietary providers, particularly through the integration of data from diverse open data sources such as Crossref, PubMed, ORCID and institutional repositories (Abramo et al., 2026; Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado, 2026).

The emergence of new data sources usually leads to comparative analyses in order to understand the differences between them and to situate them within the evolving research and infrastructure landscape (Gauch, 2023). The study by Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado (2026) provides a recent overview of comparative analyses, however, these analyses focus predominantly on WoS, Scopus, Microsoft Academic/Microsoft Academic Graph and OpenAlex. By contrast, there are comparatively few bibliometric studies that explicitly include Crossref in comparisons with other data sources.

The study by Harzing (2019) compared the quantitative coverage of Crossref and Dimensions with that of Google Scholar, Microsoft Academic, Scopus and Web of Science in terms of publication output and citation rates for the author’s publications, as well as for six leading journals in the field of Business & Economics. Harzing (2019) found that Crossref and Dimensions had higher publication coverage than Scopus and WoS for journal articles, book chapters and conference papers, but lower publication coverage than Google Scholar and Microsoft Academic across all document types considered. Harzing (2019) also found that open data sources have an advantage over proprietary data sources in terms of timeliness, as they often capture publications within a few weeks of their online publication, whilst Scopus and WoS often only index publications upon their print publication. The study by Harzing (2019) concludes that Crossref and Dimensions represent alternatives to the established commercial data sources, whereas Google Scholar and Microsoft Academic are more comprehensive open data sources.

The study by Visser et al. (2021) carried out a pair-wise comparison of Scopus with the bibliographic data sources WoS (in the version licensed by the Centre for Science and Technology Studies (CWTS), which includes the SCIE, SSCI, AHCI, CPCI indexes), Dimensions, Crossref and Microsoft Academic in terms of document coverage, as well as the completeness and accuracy of citation links. Visser et al. (2021) identified Microsoft Academic as offering the broadest coverage of documents, followed by Dimensions/Crossref, Scopus and CWTS WoS. Microsoft Academic also includes a larger number of non-scholarly documents. By contrast, Visser et al. (2021) reported that Scopus and WoS are superior to Dimensions and Microsoft Academic in terms of the completeness and accuracy of citation links. Furthermore, a structural problem was identified with Crossref, as some citation links are not openly accessible, even though they are available internally within Crossref. Visser et al. (2021) conclude that the choice of a suitable data source must be guided by the objectives of the analysis, and that both the comprehensiveness of Dimensions and Microsoft Academic and the selectivity of Scopus and WoS can offer added value.

Kramer & Jonge (2022) analysed the coverage of funding information for NWO-funded publications in Crossref, The Lens, Web of Science, Scopus and Dimensions. The analysis revealed that Crossref provided funding information for only 67% of publications, with NWO being correctly identified by its funder ID in just 45% of cases. In contrast, Web of Science (83%) and Scopus (78%) achieved a significantly higher level of completeness in funding information, as they extracted this from the full texts, whilst Crossref relied exclusively on metadata submitted by publishers.

The study by Asubiaro & Onaolapo (2023) compared the coverage of African journals in Web of Science, Scopus and Crossref. Asubiaro & Onaolapo (2023) found that African journals were covered to a significantly greater extent in Crossref than in Web of Science and Scopus. Overall, however, Asubiaro & Onaolapo (2023) assess African research as being under-represented in global citation databases.

The study by Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado (2026) combined a systematic literature review with an empirical bibliometric analysis to compare Web of Science, Scopus and OpenAlex. The literature review by Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado (2026) analysed five key areas: total volume of records, coverage of open access journals, language coverage, reference coverage, and the quality and reliability of metadata. The empirical section compared the three data sources, particularly with regard to document types, languages and research fields for publications between 2015 and 2024. Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado (2026) report that WoS and Scopus operate subscription-based business models with strictly curated, predominantly English-language content and exhibit a high degree of structural overlap between them. OpenAlex, by contrast, offers an open-source platform with heterogeneous data sources and exhibits a higher volume of records, greater linguistic and geographical diversity – particularly with regard to open access publications and publications from the Global South – as well as less overlap with the proprietary systems. With regard to metadata quality, Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado (2026) found that OpenAlex exhibits higher error rates in the identification and classification of document types, publication years, language and affiliation information than WoS and Scopus. In terms of subject areas, Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado (2026) report that WoS and Scopus have a similarly strong focus on the natural sciences, whereas OpenAlex indexes a significantly higher proportion of publications from the social sciences and humanities. Furthermore, Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado (2026) found that WoS and Scopus offer greater coverage and accuracy of references, whilst OpenAlex under-represents a considerable proportion of resources, particularly grey literature. Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado (2026) conclude that no single data source provides a comprehensive picture of the global scientific landscape and that the combined use of all three data sources can reduce disciplinary, linguistic and geographical biases.

The study by Abramo et al. (2026) compared Web of Science, Scopus, and OpenAlex—both at the individual level and at the aggregated institutional level—in terms of publication output, impact metrics, and the resulting rankings. For Italian professors and universities in the field of management engineering, the comparison showed that the consistency among the data sources was high for pure publication output but only moderate for impact-based metrics. At the institutional level, the rankings of individual universities shifted—in some cases substantially—depending on the data source used, with smaller institutions being particularly affected. Abramo et al. (2026) identified differences in disciplinary taxonomies, normalization procedures, and conventions for determining publication years as the main causes. These factors had a particular impact on rankings, especially when aggregating data at the institutional level. Abramo et al. (2026) therefore recommend triangulating results from at least two data sources in evaluations, transparently reporting the sensitivity of the results, and—when using OpenAlex—explicitly defining and communicating methodological conventions in advance, including the definition of the document types to be included.

The study by Huang, Neylon, Brookes-Kenworthy, et al. (2020) is already somewhat dated, but it is particularly interesting in the context of university rankings. Huang, Neylon, Brookes-Kenworthy, et al. (2020) compared Web of Science, Scopus, and Microsoft Academic at the institutional level and examined their impact on university rankings. The results showed that the choice of data source led to clear differences in the bibliographic characteristics examined (document coverage, publication years, document types, citation rates, open access status, affiliation assignment). Huang, Neylon, Brookes-Kenworthy, et al. (2020) found that Microsoft Academic offered the most comprehensive document coverage, particularly in the social sciences and humanities, but had less precise affiliation metadata. WoS and Scopus showed lower overall coverage but were more reliable in assigning institutions to publications. With regard to publication years, the sources showed a high degree of agreement overall, however, considerable discrepancies between the data sources occurred for individual institutions, notably those from non-English-speaking countries. Huang, Neylon, Brookes-Kenworthy, et al. (2020) reported that the proportion of open access publications at the institutional level varied depending on the source, which also led to shifts in the rankings, with universities from non-English-speaking regions being disproportionately affected. With regard to document types, it was found that Microsoft Academic captured a higher proportion of book chapters and conference papers, while Scopus frequently indexed books and monographs that were not captured by the other sources. In terms of citation rates, the study showed that Microsoft Academic tended to report lower average values, which led to clear changes in ranking positions when the data source was switched. Universities that did not achieve top positions in international rankings were particularly affected. Overall, the study showed that relying on a single data source can cause distortions in the evaluation of universities, especially for non-Western institutions and those that rank lower in international rankings. The key recommendation from Huang, Neylon, Brookes-Kenworthy, et al. (2020) was therefore to integrate multiple data sources for robust evaluation systems to enable a fair and comprehensive analysis.

The literature on the four bibliographic data sources examined – Web of Science, Scopus, Crossref, and OpenAlex – shows that all sources have advantages and disadvantages, each of which affects the evaluation of research performance and the production of university rankings.Web of Science and Scopus tend to be characterised by a high degree of structural reliability, precise affiliation assignments and more accurate citation tracking, however, they are limited by a selective content profile dominated by English-language material and by lower coverage of open access publications and research from the Global South. Crossref offers high timeliness and comprehensive coverage of publications, particularly for formats such as conference papers and book chapters, but suffers from incomplete funding information and structurally limited accessibility of citation links. OpenAlex positions itself as a comprehensive, open alternative with the largest volume of data, greater linguistic and geographical diversity, and stronger representation of research outputs from the social sciences and humanities. At the same time, OpenAlex tends to exhibit lower metadata quality, particularly in terms of document type classification, language detection and reference coverage. In addition, the studies by Huang, Neylon, Brookes-Kenworthy, et al. (2020), Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado (2026) and Abramo et al. (2026) demonstrate that the choice of data source not only influences quantitative coverage but is also decisive for the rankings of higher education institutions, specifically those from non-English-speaking regions. The results suggest that no single data source can provide a complete and fair representation of the global research landscape. Rather, the literature considers the combined use of multiple sources to be necessary in order to reduce disciplinary, linguistic and geographical biases and to create robust, transparent and fairer evaluation systems. It should also be noted, however, that the respective studies can only ever provide a snapshot of the data sources under investigation.

Open Access Data Sources

Determining the open access status of academic publications is a prerequisite for calculating open access indicators. This section provides an overview of the data sources used by the rankings examined to determine the open access status of the respective publications, as well as their specific characteristics. To this end, Table 3 first sets out below the open access data sources used by the five ranking systems examined.

Table 3 shows that a total of two different open access data sources are used: Unpaywall and OpenAlex. The SCImago Institutions Ranking, the QS Sustainability Ranking and the COKI OA Dashboard all use Unpaywall, whereas both versions of the Leiden Ranking rely on OpenAlex to determine open access status.

Unpaywall is a free, automated service that identifies open access versions of articles using DOIs. The foundations for Unpaywall were laid as early as 2011; part of the underlying technology was published in 2016, and the Unpaywall browser plug-in was released in 2017 (Else, 2018). The service exclusively records publications with Crossref DOIs and is primarily focused on journal articles and preprints (OpenAlex, n.d.-d; Unpaywall, n.d.-b). Other document types with Crossref DOIs, such as book chapters or conference papers, are in principle also processed by Unpaywall, as the genre field in the Unpaywall data schema directly adopts the Crossref type (Unpaywall, n.d.-a). However, Unpaywall itself makes no statements regarding the quality of coverage for these document types (Unpaywall, n.d.-b). In addition to publications without a Crossref DOI, so-called component DOIs are structurally not covered (OpenAlex, 2025b) – Crossref assigns these to individual components of journal articles, such as figures, tables or supplementary materials (Crossref, 2025). The OA status determination is based on several open data sources, including Crossref, DOAJ and PubMed Central. Furthermore, Unpaywall harvests over 50,000 online sources, including journals, as well as institutional and subject-specific repositories (Unpaywall, n.d.-c). Specifically, Unpaywall harvests repositories via the OAI-PMH protocol and cross-checks the metadata obtained in this way against the DOIs in its database. In addition, publishers’ landing pages are scraped directly to extract licence information not stored in Crossref. Furthermore, Unpaywall carries out manual corrections for known issues or misclassifications (OurResearch, n.d.-c). Only sources that make full texts accessible without the need to register and without CAPTCHA prompts are counted as OA sources (OpenAlex, n.d.-c). Based on this information, Unpaywall assigns an OA status to each publication, distinguishing between five categories (Unpaywall, n.d.-a): Gold, Hybrid, Green, Bronze and Closed. This classification logic follows a fixed decision-making sequence (OurResearch, n.d.-c; Unpaywall, 2021), which is schematically illustrated in Figure 5.

First, the system checks whether an article is available via open access. It then checks whether the best available version is hosted by the publisher or in a repository; if it is hosted by the publisher, the journal’s OA status and the presence of an open licence determine whether it is classified as Gold, Hybrid or Bronze. A decisive factor for the classification as Gold is whether a journal is considered to be fully open access, which Unpaywall determines on the basis of three criteria (OpenAlex, n.d.-a): Erstens gilt eine Zeitschrift als Gold-OA, wenn sie im DOAJ gelistet ist. However, as the DOAJ does not cover all Gold OA journals, secondly, journals from well-known OA publishers are classified as Gold OA via a maintained whitelist; and thirdly, Unpaywall empirically assesses whether a journal publishes exclusively OA articles. If several OA versions of an article are available, Unpaywall uses a scoring algorithm to select the best OA source, with publisher versions ranked higher than repository versions, peer-reviewed versions ranked higher than preprints, and versions with a direct PDF link ranked higher than versions without (OpenAlex, n.d.-b; OurResearch, n.d.-c). The OA status is then derived deterministically from the best OA source selected. In May 2025, Unpaywall’s underlying codebase was revised to improve speed, maintainability and data quality, and to enable community-led correction of errors (OpenAlex, 2025a). As part of this overhaul, Gold OA detection was also specifically expanded, including through the evaluation of data from OJS journals, J-STAGE and SciELO, in order to improve coverage beyond the DOAJ (OpenAlex, 2025b).

OpenAlex has already been introduced in the previous section as a bibliographic data source. As such, unlike Unpaywall, OpenAlex does not limit itself to publications with a Crossref DOI when indexing research information. Prior to the OpenAlex-Walden rewrite in November 2025, OpenAlex and Unpaywall were operated by the same organisation on separate code bases, which occasionally led to discrepancies between the OA status information provided by the two services in the context of OA status determination (OpenAlex, 2025b; Priem, 2025b). However, the OA status determination in OpenAlex followed a logic analogous to that of Unpaywall (see Figure 5) with the same five categories: Gold, Hybrid, Bronze, Green and Closed – the Diamond category was not implemented as a separate category until July 2024. Following the OpenAlex-Walden rewrite in November 2025, both systems are now operated on the same codebase, and Unpaywall has since been running as a subroutine of the OpenAlex infrastructure (OpenAlex, 2026; Priem, 2025a). The Walden source code shows that this specifically means that OpenAlex work data indexed in Crossref is extracted and converted into the Unpaywall format (OurResearch, n.d.-b), which should structurally rule out any discrepancies between the two services. The Walden pipeline determines the OA status in two stages (OurResearch, n.d.-a): First, at source level, a check is carried out to determine whether a journal is fully OA (via DOAJ, J-STAGE or observed OA rate). Building on this, each publication is assigned a status – Diamond, Gold, Hybrid, Bronze, Green or Closed – depending on accessibility, APC costs, licence and hosting location, with the best OA version being prioritised according to version type and PDF availability, in line with Unpaywall (OurResearch, n.d.-a). Since July 2024, OpenAlex has been assigning the Diamond OA status category, which identifies publications in purely open access journals without APCs. Haupka (2026) demonstrated empirically that the Walden rewrite has led to noticeable shifts in the OA classification. Diamond OA was awarded much more frequently following the rewrite, while Gold, Bronze and Hybrid OA declined. These findings highlight that individual releases on OpenAlex can have an impact on OA indicators and, at the same time, illustrate that OpenAlex is evolving dynamically (Haupka, 2026).

For rankings that use OpenAlex as their OA data source, this means that OA metrics depend not only on the underlying classification logic but also on the snapshot of data used – a limitation that must be taken into account when interpreting the results of the Leiden Ranking and the Leiden Ranking Open Edition. For rankings that use Unpaywall as a data source, the introduction of the Diamond category has no immediate impact: in the Unpaywall export, Diamond is mapped to Gold (OurResearch, n.d.-b), as the Unpaywall data schema does not provide for Diamond as a separate OA status value (Unpaywall, n.d.-a). The SCImago Institutions Ranking, the QS Sustainability Ranking and the COKI OA Dashboard therefore classify Diamond articles as Gold and are structurally unable to report the Diamond category until the Unpaywall schema is expanded accordingly. Rankings that use OpenAlex directly, on the other hand, have Diamond available as a separate category and could potentially include it in their OA indicators in the future.

Document Types

Table 4 below provides an overview of the document types covered by each ranking. It should be noted that the rankings use different type vocabularies – Crossref types for COKI, Scopus types for the SCImago Institutions Ranking and the QS Sustainability Ranking, OpenAlex types for the Leiden Ranking Open Edition, and WoS types for the traditional Leiden Ranking – meaning that nominally identical terms such as article do not necessarily describe the same content (Alperin et al., 2024; Haupka et al., 2026).

The five rankings differ noticeably in terms of the types of documents they cover. The traditional Leiden Ranking is limited to articles and reviews in international journals (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, n.d.-c). The Leiden Ranking Open Edition additionally includes book chapters in journals and book series (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, n.d.-d). The QS Sustainability Ranking takes into account not only articles and reviews but also conference papers, books, book chapters, articles in press, business articles, data papers and reports, however, it excludes editorials, letters, notes and short surveys (Quacquarelli Symonds, n.d.-e). The SCImago Institutions Ranking is the most inclusive of the Scopus-based rankings and takes into account all Scopus document types with the exception of the types retracted, erratum and preprint – including editorials, letters and notes, which QS explicitly excludes (SCImago Research Group, n.d.-a). The COKI OA Dashboard is the most inclusive overall and includes almost all Crossref document types, including monographs, book chapters, conference papers, reports and posted content – the only exclusions are datasets, databases, peer reviews, conference proceedings and a few other less traditional document types (Curtin Open Knowledge Initiative, n.d.-c).

These differences are particularly relevant for the calculation of OA indicators, as the proportion of OA publications can vary depending on the document type. Furthermore, publications without a Crossref DOI are not structurally covered by Unpaywall (OpenAlex, n.d.-d). Consequently, the reported OA proportion for rankings that use Unpaywall as their OA data source may not provide a complete picture of the OA landscape, despite broad coverage of document types. Furthermore, differences regarding the document types included can have disciplinary implications, which are examined in more detail in the following section. The inclusion of different document types could therefore systematically result in different OA percentages even for identical institutions and publication periods, which would limit the comparability of the ranking results.

Disciplinary Differences

A concern in any university evaluation is the existing disciplinary bias in large bibliographic sources used to support rankings. (Huang, Neylon, Hosking, et al., 2020b)

Disciplinary differences or biases that arise from the choice of bibliographic data sources when compiling a ranking may adversely affect the comparability of results across ranking systems by under- or over-representing different disciplines. This section examines the extent of disciplinary differences with regard to the open access dimension. However, as only the datasets from the two versions of the Leiden Ranking include disciplinary classification, the analysis is limited to the disciplinary differences between the traditional Leiden Ranking, which uses Web of Science as its bibliographic data source, and the Leiden Ranking Open Edition, which uses OpenAlex as its bibliographic data source.

To enable users to compare the results between the two versions of the Leiden Ranking, the CWTS has been providing interactive dashboards since the 2023 edition (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, n.d.-f). The results can be filtered by time period, one or more countries, fields and all indicators included in the Leiden Rankings, enabling a detailed comparison. This suggests that, across all fields, more open access information in OpenAlex leads to higher scores in the Leiden Ranking Open Edition. Furthermore, when filtering by individual subject areas, it can be seen that, with the exception of the Life and Earth Sciences subject area, OpenAlex tends to offer more open access information, this is most pronounced in the Mathematics and Computer Science and Social Sciences and Humanities subject areas. For these two fields, a higher density above the line \(x = y\) is also evident for the total number of publications (P). This suggests that, overall, OpenAlex indexes more publications in the fields Mathematics and computer science and Social sciences and humanities than Web of Science (WoS), which leads to higher scores in the Leiden Ranking Open Edition. Overall, OpenAlex – and thus the Leiden Ranking Open Edition – appears to offer better coverage of the fields Mathematics and computer science and Social sciences and humanities, while WoS – and thus the traditional Leiden Ranking – offers better coverage in the field Life and earth sciences. This is also confirmed when considering the number of open access publications for the individual open access status types (P(gold OA), P(hybrid OA), etc.).

The median absolute rank shift for Germany ranges from 2 positions in the Life and earth sciences field to 8 positions in the Physical sciences and engineering field. The 90th percentile ranges from 8.3 (All sciences) to 21.4 positions (Physical sciences and engineering). In the field Physical sciences and engineering, German institutions therefore show greater variation – both in typical cases and at the upper end of the distribution – than in other fields. The field Life and earth sciences exhibits the lowest median value among the six fields, at 2, while the 90th percentile, at 8.8, is close to the median and well below the corresponding value for Physical sciences and engineering. This suggests a comparatively even distribution, one that is not heavily influenced by individual institutions with high volatility. In the field of Mathematics and computer science, German institutions typically fluctuate by 6 positions and, in one tenth of cases, by 16.6 positions. The example for Mathematics and computer science shown in Table 6 (rank shifts of between one and five positions for the top five institutions) lies at the lower end of the distribution observed for this field.

Globally, the median ranges between 39 and 59, and the 90th percentile between 124.5 and 199.3 positions. The field Biomedical and health sciences (median 59, 90th percentile 199.3) has the highest values for both the median and the 90th percentile amongst the six fields, while All sciences (median 39, 90th percentile 124.5) has the lowest. Consequently, the field most affected by changes in ranking at global level (Biomedical and health sciences) differs from the fields most affected in Germany (Physical sciences and engineering, Mathematics and computer science).

One possible explanation for the comparatively significant shifts in rankings in Mathematics and computer science and Physical sciences and engineering for German institutions lies in the methodological definition of core publications in both versions of the Leiden Ranking. The methodology of the traditional Leiden Ranking defines core publications for WoS exclusively as the document types article and review. By contrast, the definition used for OpenAlex is less restrictive (van Eck & Waltman, 2025). The reason for this is that, compared with proprietary databases, OpenAlex uses a significantly less granular document-type classification. Whereas WoS lists conference papers as a separate document type, clearly distinguished from article/review, OpenAlex groups journal articles, conference papers and preprints into a single article type as part of its document type classification; in OpenAlex, article effectively functions as a catch-all category (Alperin et al., 2024; Haupka et al., 2026). This lower level of granularity has direct consequences for the core-publications count. Conference papers, which are excluded in WoS via explicit type assignment, are implicitly assigned to the generic article type in OpenAlex. Depending on the field and publication culture, this could result in differing counts, and fields characterised by the high significance of conference papers as a publication format might thus exhibit greater disciplinary differences. This also suggests that even standardised, size-independent indicators such as PP(OA) are not independent of data sources. This must be taken into account when comparing different ranking versions and interpreting their results.

It should be noted, however, that OpenAlex has since revised its own document type classification (Demes, 2026). OpenAlex now categorises 25 different document types and, according to its own statements, the document type of around 10% of the indexed works have changed (Demes, 2026). This will have a particular impact on future versions of the rankings that use OpenAlex as a data source.

Open Access Indicators

Within evaluative bibliometrics, so-called productivity indicators focus on counting publication volumes in order to quantify the productivity of individuals, institutions or countries (Gauch, 2023). In this context, open access indicators can be understood as a form of productivity indicator, as they usually count the number of open access publications and use this to generate an approximate measure of the productivity of researchers, institutions or countries in the area of open access.

In line with good bibliometric practice, as outlined, for example, in the Leiden Manifesto (Hicks et al., 2015), the selection of indicators used in the context of rankings requires careful consideration of their suitability for comparing institutions. A comparison between institutions from different regional contexts or with varying numbers of researchers and publication volumes can be problematic if the indicators merely represent absolute numbers of publications, as the specific conditions and challenges of each institution are not taken into account. To improve comparability between institutions, various forms of normalisation are therefore applied when constructing indicators (Gauch, 2023). One form of normalisation with regard to open access indicators could be to set the absolute number of an institution’s open access publications in relation to the absolute number of all its publications during the period under review.

In addition to individual indicators, so-called composite indicators are also used for certain rankings that are structured as league tables. These indicators are derived from weighted aggregates of individual indicators (Glänzel & Debackere, 2009; Johnes, 2018). The idea behind composite indicators is to capture complex phenomena relating to scientific activities by combining and consolidating various indicators into a single, comprehensive metric. These linearised metrics, which can be ranked, make it easier to compare institutions, individuals or countries (Gauch, n.d.). Nevertheless, certain problems are associated with composite indicators – a good overview can be found in Forthmann et al. (2024). Among the problems and points of criticism are, for example, the fact that the selection of weightings for individual components in composite indicators is often opaque and, in some cases, arbitrary. Yet the weights of the individual components have a huge impact on the resulting ranking (Forthmann et al., 2024; Glänzel & Debackere, 2009). Furthermore, criticism has been raised that there is often a lack of sufficient transparency regarding the construction of these indicators, which can make it difficult to interpret the ranking results (Forthmann et al., 2024).

Table 8 provides a summary of the key differences between the five ranking systems in terms of the open access indicators they use.

Both versions of the Leiden Ranking essentially provide two types of open access indicators: 1) indicators that show the total volume of open access publications produced by a higher education institution over a four-year period, and 2) indicators that show the proportion of open access publications out of the total publication volume of a higher education institution over a four-year period. Both indicators are reported once as a total for open access publications and, in addition, separately for the common open access status types (gold, hybrid, green, bronze, and unknown as used in the traditional Leiden Ranking). In addition to the open access indicators, the total publication volume per institution is also stated. Although a comparison between institutions based on absolute numbers has its limitations, it can still be useful to report absolute figures in order to improve the transparency and traceability of the indicators. Stating the absolute numbers enables institutions to verify the percentage figures and assess their accuracy.

Similar to the two Leiden Rankings, the COKI OA Dashboard also reports both absolute figures and relative proportions in relation to an institution’s open access publication volume. The scope of the indicators shown in the COKI OA Dashboard varies between the institutional overview page and the detailed pages for individual institutions. The overview page, which is designed to enable a comparison of all institutions represented in the COKI OA Dashboard, displays the following indicators for the publication period 2000–2024: 1) the percentage of open access publications, 2) a stacked bar chart showing the percentages of articles that are available via the publisher, other platforms, both, or not at all via open access. However, the bar chart does not show the respective percentages; 3) the total number of publications by an institution; and 4) the total number of open access publications by an institution. The detail pages for individual institutions contain further specific indicators that provide a more detailed overview of an institution’s open access publishing activity. Among other things, these pages display percentage figures for the stacked bar chart and trends over time. It is striking that there is a discrepancy between an institution’s overview page and its details page regarding the absolute figures for total publication volume and open access publication volume. The overview page shows the exact figures, whereas the details page displays rounded figures. Furthermore, the percentage of open access publications shown on both pages is a rounded figure. Although the use of absolute figures, similar to the Leiden Rankings, ensures greater verifiability of the indicators, the presentation of rounded figures is less precise.

The SCImago Institutions Ranking uses a composite indicator that aggregates various indicators from the three areas of Research, Innovation and Societal. The Research dimension accounts for 50% of the composite indicator and is therefore clearly more heavily weighted than the other two dimensions, which are weighted at 30% (Innovation) and 20% (Societal). In the SCImago Institutions Ranking, the open access indicator forms part of the Research dimension and is weighted at 2%. It is therefore one of the least heavily weighted indicators within the Research dimension and the composite indicator as a whole. The open access indicator represents the percentage of publications that are published in open access journals or indexed in Unpaywall (SCImago Research Group, n.d.-a). However, the SCImago Institutions Ranking does not report either the open access indicator or the total score achieved by the institutions, it merely states the resulting rank.

Similar to the SCImago Institutions Ranking, the QS Sustainability Ranking also uses a composite indicator. To this end, the QS Sustainability Ranking aggregates various indicators from the areas of Environmental Impact, Social Impact and Governance. The Governance dimension accounts for 10% of the composite indicator and is therefore given less weight than the other two dimensions, each of which is weighted at 45%. The open access indicator forms part of the Governance dimension in the QS Sustainability Ranking and accounts for 1% of this dimension. All indicators within the Governance dimension are assigned the same weighting and are therefore given equal consideration for the dimension. The open access indicator specifies the proportion of an institution’s total research output that is available in open access according to Unpaywall (Quacquarelli Symonds, n.d.-b). In constructing the indicator, a distinction is made between different types of open access status (gold, hybrid, green) and subject areas, to which different weightings are assigned. The final open access indicator is aggregated based on the individual values per field and scaled to a range between 0 and 100 (Quacquarelli Symonds, n.d.-b). In addition to the total score, the QS Sustainability Ranking dataset shows the score achieved for each dimension separately. The total score is only shown up to rank 902.

Overall, it is evident that the five ranking systems differ, in some cases considerably, in their use of open access indicators, even though all the open access indicators outlined measure and present the productivity of institutions in relation to open access. Differences and challenges regarding the comparability of ranking results arise, for example, from the consideration of different types of open access status (gold, hybrid, green, etc.) for classifying open access publications. This also results in a path dependency on the data source used to identify open access publications. Both versions of the Leiden Ranking use OpenAlex as their data source for identifying open access publications and, furthermore, report the open access indicators separately for all open access status types. The QS Sustainability Ranking, on the other hand, uses Unpaywall as its data source, but appears to distinguish only between gold, hybrid and green open access publications (Quacquarelli Symonds, n.d.-b). The SCImago Institutions Ranking also uses Unpaywall as its data source, but does not appear to distinguish between different types of open access status (SCImago Research Group, n.d.-a). The COKI OA Dashboard likewise uses Unpaywall as its data source for identifying open access publications, but, unlike the other rankings, does not display the typical open access status types with their corresponding colours. Instead, the COKI OA Dashboard groups gold, hybrid and bronze publications together under the category Publisher Open. These differences make it difficult to compare the ranking systems in terms of open access.

Due to the more nuanced presentation of the indicators, both versions of the Leiden Ranking and the COKI OA Dashboard require users to examine the individual dimensions separately in order to carry out a comprehensive assessment of the institutions. This entails a higher level of complexity when interpreting the results. The SCImago Institutions Ranking and the QS Sustainability Ranking simplify this complexity through the use of composite indicators. However, the composite indicators prove to be less suitable for open access monitoring and the assessment of open access activities, as the specific open access-related values cannot be derived directly from the composite indicators. Furthermore, open access indicators play a secondary role in the SCImago Institutions Ranking and the QS Sustainability Ranking, as they each account for only a very small proportion of the overall score.

Institutional Participation in Data Collection

This section examines the data collection methods used by the various ranking providers and explores the extent to which institutions can participate in the data collection process that concerns them.

For the traditional Leiden Ranking, the CWTS does not collect any data directly from the ranked institutions (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, n.d.-e; Van Eck, 2025c). The ranking is based on data from the Web of Science, which is processed and standardised by the CWTS. However, as Web of Science is a proprietary data source subject to licensing restrictions, the CWTS is unable to make the raw data or the curated data openly accessible (van Eck, Visser, et al., 2024). Consequently, it is not readily possible for the institutions concerned to verify the quality of the data.

Similarly, no data is collected directly from the institutions for the Leiden Ranking Open Edition (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, n.d.-h). However, unlike the traditional Leiden Ranking, the Leiden Ranking Open Edition draws on open data sources, such as OpenAlex and ROR, to produce the ranking (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, n.d.-b). Through the use of open data sources and thanks to the provision of raw and results data by the CWTS (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, n.d.-f), the ranked institutions are able, on the one hand, to verify the quality of the data and, on the other hand, to participate in the data curation process by reporting any data quality issues to the relevant stakeholders (van Eck, Visser, et al., 2024).

The methodology of the SCImago Institutions Ranking states that data for the ranking is collected from various sources, namely: Scopus, PATSTAT, Google, Semrush, Unpaywall, PlumX Metrics, Mendeley and Overton (SCImago Research Group, n.d.-a). The methodology makes no mention of any opportunity for institutional involvement in data collection, it is therefore assumed that no data is collected directly from the ranked institutions. The bibliographic data for the SCImago Institutions Ranking is retrieved from Scopus, a proprietary data source which, like Web of Science, is subject to licensing restrictions. The raw data for the SCImago Institutions Ranking is not publicly available, meaning that it can be assumed that ranked institutions do not readily have the opportunity to verify or correct the data relating to them before it is included in the ranking. To determine open access status, the SCImago Institutions Ranking uses Unpaywall, an open data source. As with the Leiden Ranking Open Edition, it may be possible for the institutions concerned to participate in the data curation process for the open access data by reporting data quality issues directly to Unpaywall. Overall, the methodology of the SCImago Institutions Ranking provides little information regarding data quality and possible limitations, nor is there any transparent communication regarding the options available to institutions for helping to improve data quality.

The COKI Open Access Dashboard exclusively uses open data sources for data collection, including Crossref, Unpaywall and ROR (Curtin Open Knowledge Initiative, n.d.-c). The methodology of the COKI OA Dashboard describes the process of data collection from external data sources in detail, but provides no information regarding data provision by the ranked institutions themselves (Curtin Open Knowledge Initiative, n.d.-c). It can therefore be assumed that there is no direct institutional involvement in the data collection process. As the COKI dataset is based on open data sources, it can further be assumed – as with the Leiden Ranking Open Edition – that the institutions concerned have the opportunity to participate indirectly in the data curation process by reporting data quality issues directly to the relevant data providers. Additionally, COKI communicates the limitations of its own dataset (Curtin Open Knowledge Initiative, n.d.-c) and provides various contact options (Curtin Open Knowledge Initiative, n.d.-a), so that institutions also have the opportunity to play an active role in contributing to the improvement of the data in the COKI dataset.

The QS Sustainability Ranking is the only one of the five ranking systems examined that directly involves the institutions concerned in the data collection process. The methodology of the QS Sustainability Ranking states that QS generally encourages all institutions to provide data for the QS rankings (Quacquarelli Symonds, n.d.-g). QS provides a dedicated platform for data submission, however, the methodology does not provide a precise list of the data requested (Quacquarelli Symonds, n.d.-g). Should institutions that are eligible for inclusion in the QS Sustainability Ranking fail to provide data, QS assesses these institutions on the basis of proprietary and publicly available data, or data previously provided by the institution in question. A complete opt-out from the ranking by institutions is therefore not provided for. QS justifies this by arguing that the inclusion of all qualifying institutions enhances the reliability of the rankings (Quacquarelli Symonds, n.d.-c). However, QS does not provide any information on how many of the ranked institutions actually take up the opportunity to submit data.

Overall, it appears that institutions only have limited opportunities to participate in the data collection process carried out by ranking providers. The QS Sustainability Ranking is the only ranking system that collects data directly from the institutions concerned, although it is not entirely clear what specific data is requested from them. The other ranking systems rely exclusively on data from external sources that are not collected directly from the institutions. However, the use of open data sources, such as in the Leiden Ranking Open Edition or the COKI OA Dashboard, can offer institutions an opportunity to improve the quality of the data relating to them. High-quality source data and transparent methodologies for compiling rankings can also help to improve confidence in the reliability and validity of the ranking systems.

Limitations

This analysis is subject to several methodological limitations, which must be taken into account when interpreting the results.

The analysis of institutional overlap between the five ranking systems was restricted to German institutions due to incomplete ROR-ID mappings for the SCImago Institutions Ranking and the QS Sustainability Ranking. The reported overlap percentages cannot therefore be readily extrapolated to the global level.

The analysis of disciplinary differences is limited to the two versions of the Leiden Ranking, as only these include disciplinary classifications in their data. It is not possible, on this basis, to draw conclusions regarding disciplinary biases in the other three ranking systems. The analysis of changes in ranking between the traditional Leiden Ranking and the Leiden Ranking Open Edition relates exclusively to the subset of institutions that are common to both, as identified by ROR IDs. For Germany, 15 of the 73 institutions included in the Leiden Ranking Open Edition were not represented in the traditional Leiden Ranking and were therefore excluded from the analysis. Furthermore, the absolute median and percentile values between the German (N = 53–58) and global samples (N = 1,207–1,586) were not directly comparable due to the considerable differences in sample size. Corresponding comparisons were therefore limited to qualitative observations within the respective levels.

All observations reported in this analysis represent snapshots of the respective underlying data versions. The same applies to the literature used regarding the bibliographic data sources. The studies reflect the state of Web of Science, Scopus, Crossref and OpenAlex at the time each analysis was conducted, and, due to the dynamic evolution of these data sources, the study results cannot simply be extrapolated to the current state.

Summary

A comparative analysis of the five ranking systems, focusing on the open access dimension, shows that, despite a common interest – the measurement of open access activities at research institutions – the OA indicators used are only comparable to a limited extent. The main reasons for this limited comparability lie, in particular, in the following areas:

  1. In the choice of bibliographic data source (Web of Science, Scopus, OpenAlex, Crossref), which determines both the coverage of publications and their disciplinary and geographical distribution.
  2. In the choice of OA data source (Unpaywall vs. OpenAlex), which defines the available OA status categories and their classification logic.
  3. In the document types included, which define the denominator for all relative OA indicators.
  4. In the construction of the indicators themselves, which ranges from differentiated individual indicators to highly aggregated composite indicators.
  5. In empirically observable differences between the rankings themselves: the systems examined differ considerably in the number and overlap of the institutions included (in particular, the COKI OA Dashboard shows much broader coverage than the other four rankings) as well as in the geographical representation of individual countries (see Table 1). Using the two versions of the Leiden Ranking as an example, it was also found that the ranking position of individual institutions can shift noticeably depending on the data source used, and that the extent of this shift varies between disciplines as well as between Germany and the global level.

Consequently, both a direct comparison of the ranking results of individual institutions across the five systems and a comparison of their respective open access activities across multiple ranking systems are of limited value. Neither the underlying publication volumes nor the calculated open access proportions relate to comparable populations. Furthermore, institutions not included in a particular ranking cannot, in principle, use it to assess their own standing, whereas institutions featured in multiple rankings cannot have their respective results directly compared.

This has specific implications for users who wish to utilise rankings for open access monitoring or strategic decision-making. Rankings based on open data sources – in particular the Leiden Ranking Open Edition and the COKI OA Dashboard – offer greater transparency and traceability, as well as the opportunity to report data quality issues to the relevant entities. This offers advantages over ranking systems that rely on proprietary data sources. However, transparency alone does not resolve the fundamental problem of comparability between ranking systems. Rankings using composite indicators, such as the SCImago Institutions Ranking and the QS Sustainability Ranking, are generally less suitable for nuanced OA monitoring, as the OA indicators each account for only a very small proportion of the overall score and no disaggregated OA figures are reported.
